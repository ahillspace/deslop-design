# Long-form, printable, and presentation documents

Use this reference for a report, memo, article, decision rationale, long-form explanation, PDF, printable document, or narrative slide deck when argument, evidence, reading order, or page composition matters.
Combine it with the route-specific genre, copy, data, visual-craft, semantics, and QA references that apply.

## Match the document to its use

Classify both medium and purpose.
A decision report helps readers assess evidence or choose; an explanatory document builds understanding; a memo accounts for a choice; a live deck guides a spoken sequence; a self-guided deck must carry its own context.
An HTML report has a screen reading route and may also have print output.
A PDF or office document has page composition and a reading structure that must be inspected separately when tools allow.
Do not turn every artifact into a landing page or consulting deck.

Identify the reader's actual question and the scope of the request.
Build a compact claim ledger for consequential statements: location, source, scope/date/population/unit, qualification, and status as fact, interpretation, recommendation, or unknown.
Trace figures, quotations, citations, causal wording, legal text, and source links to supplied material.
Keep limitations beside the claim they qualify.
Never upgrade an estimate into a result, association into causation, plan into completed work, or sample into a population finding.

## Build a legible argument

Write a short outline that follows the reasoning: supported answer or decision, necessary context, evidence, interpretation, trade-offs or alternatives where real, limitations, method, and implication.
Put prerequisite definitions before dependent claims.
Place evidence before or beside the inference it supports.
Lead with the conclusion when readers need a decision and the evidence supports a concise answer; preserve narrative order when it is needed to understand the subject.
Remove repeated setup only after checking that it adds no separate scope, exception, or voice.

Make headings carry the argument when scanned.
Name the question, claim, finding, or action of each section, not its visual container.
Keep semantic heading levels aligned with actual nesting; do not choose an HTML heading solely to obtain a font size.
Use navigation, anchors, bookmarks, section numbers, footnote returns, and appendices only where they improve retrieval and have working destinations.

Choose the visual form for the evidence.
Use prose for one relationship, tables for exact records or many cross-row comparisons, and charts when the visual comparison answers a question.
Introduce each figure where it matters, write a caption that states what it shows and under what limits, preserve source attribution, and give an appropriate textual equivalent.
Avoid a summary box or callout that separates a claim from its qualification.
Use speaker notes or appendix content for material that supports the argument without crowding a live presentation; the main claim still needs enough evidence to remain credible.

## Adapt the page and export

Treat screen, print, and fixed-page export as distinct outputs.
For HTML, inspect rendered reading order at wide and narrow widths, including figures, table relationships, footnotes, links, and the DOM sequence when columns or visual ordering change.
For a print or PDF deliverable, use the actual target page size and inspect the generated pages or export.
Do not claim export behavior from source CSS alone.

Check page margins, clipping, contrast in grayscale, font substitution, running headers and footers, page numbers, link destinations, repeated table headings, and whether figures stay near captions and source notes.
Use page-break rules to keep a heading with enough following text to identify its section and to keep small inseparable units together where possible.
Check widows and orphans as reading interruptions rather than enforcing a fixed numeric count on every layout.
Avoid splitting a short caption or qualification from its figure or table; do not force an entire long section onto one page when that creates empty space.
Keep page context useful without adding decorative furniture.

For a narrative deck, test the title sequence as an outline and the live or self-guided path appropriate to its use.
Check slide hierarchy, data legibility, source notes, progression, transitions, and backup material.
Do not make one idea per page a rule when connected evidence must be read together.

If the supplied artifact is only a screenshot or fixed PDF, do not claim editable-source access or proven keyboard behavior.
Preserve the original artifact, report visible findings, and name the source or export needed to verify or implement an edit.
Use [reading semantics and accessibility](accessibility.md) for semantic structure and applicable standards.

## Example: protect a report's claim

Suppose the source says: “In a review of 18 requests, 11 had different statuses in the intake form and dispatch log. Four records may be duplicates. The review did not test whether status mismatch caused delay.”

A misleading redesign might headline “61% of requests were delayed,” chart an invented delay trend, and move the possible-duplicate note to an appendix.
A faithful opening says that the sources disagreed in 11 of 18 reviewed requests, keeps the possible duplicates and untested causality beside that statement, and labels a combined view as a proposed follow-up.
If no visual comparison adds information, retain the sentence and omit the chart.
The figures are an illustrative fixture, not an observed result.

## Verify before delivery

Compare the source and revised document for claims, values, units, dates, quotes, citations, links, caveats, sections, and behavior.
For screen work, inspect opening, middle, end, long content, figures, tables, narrow view, and any interactive states.
For print or PDF, inspect actual pages at the intended size and check pagination, margins, headings, running elements, figures, tables, links, and structure or extraction order where available.
For a deck, inspect the complete sequence and the appropriate spoken or self-guided context.
Report exactly which checks were source inspection, render inspection, export inspection, keyboard or assistive-technology checks, automated checks, or reader testing.
Do not treat a polished cover, successful export, or attractive first viewport as proof of the complete document.
