# Compare meaningful redesign directions

Use this reference when more than one plausible way to organize the reader's task, argument, or interaction could change the outcome.
The current artifact is an incumbent option.
Skip this route for audit-only work, a narrow correction, a settled system with no meaningful choice, or an obvious content-preserving repair.

## Define the decision before styling

State one decision question that alternatives can resolve.
Derive thresholds and trade-offs from the brief, source content, system rules, and task.
For example, preserving a caveat beside its claim may be a threshold; surfacing a supported recommendation sooner may be a trade-off.
Do not use “modern,” “beautiful,” or “better” as criteria unless the brief makes them observable.

Generate the smallest set that tests a real assumption; two often suffice.
Each direction must answer the same reader task and preserve the same required evidence, actions, and scope.
Make alternatives structurally distinct: conclusion-led versus evidence-led, one complete task versus staged steps, overview versus a focused decision, or reader comparison versus an inspectable system recommendation.
A palette, font, or card treatment alone does not make a new concept unless visual character is itself the decision.
Treat the incumbent as an option and avoid building every direction to production fidelity.

Use comparable lightweight representations such as outlines, annotated layouts, a representative HTML slice, or one form path with an error state.
For each, state the organizing idea, reader sequence, assumption tested, shared content, difference, likely benefit, cost, and uncertainty.
Do not create a polished full-page concept beside a sparse outline that cannot be compared fairly.

## Decide and carry learning forward

Apply the same thresholds and trade-offs to every option, including the existing artifact.
Prefer evidence from source constraints, rendered representative views, a task walkthrough, or authorized user research over taste polling.
Do not describe expert judgment as user validation.
If one option violates a threshold, explain the evidence and remove it from consideration.
If important trade-offs point in different directions and the brief does not rank them, report the conflict instead of inventing a blended winner.
When the decision is reversible, recommend a provisional default and state what evidence could change it.

Record why the chosen direction fits, what the strongest rejected option taught, what it costs, what remains uncertain, and when to revisit it.
Do not build a feature pile from every rejected option.
After selection, use the applicable craft and genre references to refine one direction and verify the reason it won.

### Example: two report openings

An incident report has a date, impact count, qualified causal finding, timeline, remediation, and source links.
The choice is whether to open with a concise qualified conclusion or with a source-linked chronology.
Both concepts keep the same count, date, caveat, remediation, and links; they differ in reading order and what a hurried reader sees first.
Compare equal-fidelity outlines or a small representative HTML slice, then inspect whether the caveat stays attached to the finding.
Choose a provisional route from the task and its thresholds; do not claim that users preferred it unless actual user evidence exists.

## Verify the selected direction

Check the same evidence and task used to decide, plus required content and behavior, relevant states, narrow reflow, keyboard and reading order, and print when applicable.
For a concept audit, report the decision question, options and fidelity, criteria, evidence, recommendation or unresolved conflict, costs, and limitations.
For audit-only work, report alternatives as recommendations without implementing them.
